Razor vs. Shear vs. Score Slitting: Choosing the Right Knife for Film and Foil

A slitting blade cannot be selected from its diameter or material grade alone. The first question is how the machine cuts the moving web. Razor, shear and score (also called crush) slitting use different cutting actions. A knife that is appropriate for one arrangement may be unsuitable for another, even when both machines process plastic film.

This guide explains the three methods, their limitations, and the information a converter should check when ordering a replacement blade. It is general selection guidance: the machine manual and the approved knife drawing take precedence over any generic recommendation.

These are methods, not three interchangeable blade shapes. In particular, the common razor-slitting setup often uses a thin, straight replaceable razor rather than a circular knife. Do not order a circular slitter simply because the material is described as “film.”

1. Razor slitting: a stationary edge for suitable thin webs

Razor slitting places a sharp stationary edge in the path of a moving web. In a razor-in-air arrangement the blade acts on a supported or tensioned web without a receiving groove underneath; in razor-in-groove slitting the blade cuts over a grooved roll. The correct arrangement depends on the equipment and the web.

Razor slitting can be useful for certain thin, flexible films when the web path, tension and blade exposure are properly controlled. It is not a universal answer for every plastic film or foil: abrasive fillers, multilayer structures, web instability and material buildup can change the result. A blade that cuts a thin film cleanly on one line may cause tearing or premature wear on another.

For replacement: confirm the razor's exact length, width, thickness, mounting format and compatible holder. Also check blade orientation and whether the process uses a groove or another web-support arrangement. Safe handling matters: razor edges are sharp even when the machine is stopped.

2. Shear slitting: matched rotary knives

Shear slitting uses an upper and lower circular cutting element in a controlled, scissors-like arrangement. This method is used for a wide range of converting materials, including paper and certain films, foils and laminates. With the right geometry and setup it can produce a consistent slit edge, but knife condition is only one variable.

A matched shear system depends on the relationship between the upper and lower tools. Knife overlap, side loading, runout, arbor fit and web handling all influence performance. The machine maker's prescribed setup should be followed; there is no single overlap or side-load value that is correct for every slitter.

When sourcing a replacement, inspect more than the outer diameter:

  • Outer diameter (OD), inner diameter (ID/bore) and body thickness
  • Flat or dish profile; single or double bevel where applicable
  • Which part is the upper knife and which is the lower knife/ring
  • Seating face, mounting method and any locating features
  • Edge condition, allowable regrind limits and approved material

HK BladeTech's online catalog includes circular slitter knife listings, including a Tidland-compatible Class II shear slitter blade. A compatible-style listing is not proof of fit for every machine revision. Confirm the exact part number and drawing before ordering.

3. Score or crush slitting: a circular knife against an anvil

In many converting applications, score slitting and crush slitting describe a knife pressing the web against a hardened anvil roll. The localized pressure parts the material. Some manufacturers distinguish scoring (marking or partial penetration) from crush cutting (full separation), so state which result your process requires rather than relying on the name alone.

This method may be considered for certain paper, nonwoven, adhesive, foam or other compressible substrates. However, material response varies: a setup that works for one laminate could produce edge deformation, debris or inconsistent widths with another. A radiused or otherwise specified knife edge may be required; substituting a sharp shear knife without checking the design can damage the cutting system.

For a replacement, confirm the knife's edge radius or profile, OD, bore, thickness, holder interface and the condition and material of the mating anvil. Review any specified operating pressure with the equipment manufacturer.

Why blade material alone does not determine cut quality

D2, HSS and carbide-based constructions may all be encountered in industrial slitting, but a higher nominal hardness does not guarantee a cleaner cut or longer life. Wear resistance, chip resistance, edge finish and resharpening requirements interact with web contamination, speed and the machine's cutting method.

A useful selection sequence is:

  1. Identify the cutting method and knife position. Razor, upper shear, lower shear and score knife are distinct requests.
  2. Confirm the original geometry. Use the machine manual, OEM number, drawing or approved replacement sample.
  3. Describe the substrate. Material, thickness or basis weight, coating, adhesives, fillers and any abrasive content.
  4. Define the problem to solve. Edge fuzz, dust, burrs, web tearing, buildup, inconsistent slit width or frequent changeovers.
  5. Review the material and coating only after fit is established. Ask for an appropriate proposal rather than selecting by hardness alone.
