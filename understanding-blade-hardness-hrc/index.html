Understanding Blade Hardness: What HRC Means for Your Plastic Recycling Machine

An HRC value appears on many replacement granulator and shredder knife specifications. It is useful—but it is not a complete measure of quality, expected service life or toughness. Two blades with similar HRC readings can behave very differently because their steel, microstructure, edge geometry and operating conditions differ.

This guide explains what Rockwell C hardness actually measures, how to interpret a specified range and what to check before ordering industrial knives.

What is Rockwell C hardness?

Rockwell C (HRC) is an indentation hardness scale commonly used for hardened steels. In the standard Rockwell C test, a diamond-cone indenter is subjected to a 10 kgf preliminary test force and a 150 kgf total test force. The hardness number is derived from the permanent change in indentation depth under the prescribed test cycle.

A higher HRC reading generally indicates greater resistance to this specific indentation test. It does not directly measure abrasive wear rate, tensile strength, fracture toughness or hours of blade service.

A useful hardness report therefore needs more than a single number: the scale, specified target or range, suitable test surface and location, applicable test method, and part or sample identification.

For a given steel, pushing toward a higher hardness condition may reduce the tolerance for certain types of impact or edge chipping. The relationship is not a universal one-for-one trade-off, because microstructure, tempering treatment and edge design also matter.

Why a higher HRC is not always better for a granulator

Consider two operating conditions:

Relatively consistent, abrasive feedstock. Wear resistance may be an important priority, and hardness is one selection variable. Abrasives, fillers and edge design must still be considered.

Feedstock containing occasional hard contaminants. Impact and edge chipping may dominate. Choosing the highest possible HRC without addressing contaminants, clearance or edge support can make the outcome worse.

In both cases, the acceptable hardness must be judged against the OEM specification or approved blade drawing and the actual material/heat-treatment capability.

What about D2 hardness ranges?

AISI D2 and related cold-work tool-steel products are often specified at relatively high hardness. You may see illustrative ranges such as 58–62 HRC in industry literature, but that is not a universal target for every granulator or shredder knife. The right range depends on the steel grade, blade section, heat-treatment schedule, bevel, duty and the machine manufacturer's requirements.

Never revise a specified hardness merely because another website lists a higher value. If a supplier proposes a different range, ask for the technical rationale and the effect on the approved part specification.

Can tungsten carbide be compared using HRC?

Not straightforwardly. Cemented carbides are commonly characterised using hardness methods such as Vickers (HV) or Rockwell A (HRA), selected for the material and test requirements. An HRC value quoted for a hardened steel should not be compared directly with an HRA or HV value as though they were the same scale.

Material grade and carbide binder also influence cutting behaviour. For context, read our D2 versus tungsten carbide guide.

What to ask for when specifying hardness

Before placing an order, agree on the material grade, target hardness or allowable range (if applicable), reference drawing, required test method and location, and whether a material or inspection record is needed. Not every blade geometry provides a suitable direct test surface; the inspection approach should be agreed in advance.

Avoid quoting only “maximum HRC” on the enquiry. The real objective is a knife suitable for its machine and operating conditions.

Send the part number, drawing and hardness requirements for review. We can discuss the requested specification without promising that a single hardness number guarantees blade life.
